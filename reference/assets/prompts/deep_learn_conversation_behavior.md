## Task 1.1: The Right Triangle Foundation

Before we can look at proofs, we need to be experts on the "star of the show": the **Right Triangle**. Every visual proof we study relies on the specific way this triangle is built.

A right triangle is defined by having exactly one angle that measures $90^\circ$ (a right angle), which looks like the corner of a square. This corner is the anchor for the entire theorem. <span class="memory-ref" data-type="profileMemory">(Tailored for high school geometry basics)</span>

<div content-section="definition">
    <p>In a right triangle, we give specific names to the three sides based on their relationship to the $90^\circ$ angle:</p>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Legs ($a$ and $b$):</strong> These are the two sides that meet to form the right angle. They are perpendicular to each other.</li>
        <li><strong>Hypotenuse ($c$):</strong> This is the longest side of the triangle, located directly across from the right angle. It "connects" the two legs.</li>
    </ul>
</div>

<diagram data-placeholder-id="dg_b0e2a0abf898" data-subtype="gemini_image" data-layout="right" data-status="pending" data-caption="Anatomy of a Right Triangle"></diagram>

Understanding these labels is crucial because the Pythagorean Theorem describes a relationship between the squares of these specific lengths. 

<div content-section="key_points">
    <ul>
        <li>The <strong>right angle</strong> is usually marked with a small square symbol.</li>
        <li>The <strong>hypotenuse</strong> is always the longest side.</li>
        <li>It doesn't matter which leg you call $a$ or $b$, but the <strong>hypotenuse must always be $c$</strong>.</li>
    </ul>
</div>

## Task 1.1: The Area Relationship ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$)

Now that we know the anatomy of a right triangle, let’s look at the actual "magic" of the Pythagorean Theorem. The formula $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ isn't just about numbers; it represents physical **space**.

When we say "$a$ squared" ($a^2$), we are literally talking about the area of a square where every side has a length of $a$. Imagine drawing a square attached to each side of our right triangle.

<div content-section="core_equations">
    <p>The Pythagorean Theorem states:</p>
    <tex>$$Area_a + Area_b = Area_c$$</tex>
    <p>Where:</p>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Area A:</strong> The square built on leg $a$.</li>
        <li><strong>Area B:</strong> The square built on leg $b$.</li>
        <li><strong>Area C:</strong> The square built on the hypotenuse $c$.</li>
    </ul>
</div>

<diagram data-placeholder-id="dg_022084b4e6d6" data-subtype="gemini_image" data-layout="right" data-status="pending" data-caption="Visualizing the Pythagorean Theorem as areas"></diagram>

The total amount of "paper" needed to cover the two smaller squares (on sides $a$ and $b$) is exactly the same amount of "paper" needed to cover the big square on the longest side ($c$).

<div content-section="important_takeaways">
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Geometry is Algebra:</strong> The "exponent 2" in the formula literally refers to the two dimensions of a square.</li>
        <li><strong>The Hypotenuse Wins:</strong> Because $c$ is the longest side, its square will always have the largest area, equaling the sum of the other two.</li>
    </ul>
</div>
